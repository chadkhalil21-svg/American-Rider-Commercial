## Study literature, carefully interpreted
1. Heskett, Jones, Loveman, Sasser & Schlesinger (1994), *Putting the Service-Profit Chain to Work*, Harvard Business Review. Investment in front-line capability and service supports a service-performance framework. https://hbr.org/1994/03/putting-the-service-profit-chain-to-work-2
2. Rosemary Batt (2002), *Managing Customer Services: Human Resource Practices, Quit Rates, and Sales Growth*, Academy of Management Journal. Observational study finds lower quit rates and higher sales growth in service organizations emphasizing skills, participation and relative compensation. Association is not causality proven for a TNC. https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/3069383
3. Zeynep Ton (2020), *If Higher Pay Is Profitable, Why Is It So Rare? Modeling Competing Strategies in Mass Market Services*, Organization Science. Model shows complementarity of richer service work and compensation, not proof that paying any particular percentage produces a particular customer outcome. https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2019.1347
4. Kapoor (2020), *Inefficient Incentives and Nonprice Allocations: Experimental Evidence from Big-Box Restaurants*, Journal of Economics & Management Strategy. Field experiment found some incentive design changes improved customer wait times and earnings with no discernible cost in perceived quality in its study. Helpful context, not causal proof for American Rider or a recommended public-site comparison. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jems.12346

**Public-facing research style:** Refer respectfully to service professionals, compensation, continuing qualifications, reliable assistance and customer experiences. Do not mention minimum-wage arguments, low-paid labor, employment class comparisons or claims to have already transformed the industry. In fact, independent transportation Operators are not necessarily employees; this research is relevant context rather than a one-to-one pay policy validation.
